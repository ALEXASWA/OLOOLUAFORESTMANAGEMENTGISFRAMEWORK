## 1.0 Introduction

The United Nations Human Settlements Programme (UN-Habitat) research on Africa's fastest-growing cities in 2021 ranked Nairobi among the top ten, at ninth position after Kinshasa, Addis Ababa, Lagos, and Accra among others. This is attributed to rapid urban population growth due to increased rural-urban migration, high fertility rates, and improved healthcare service provision within the city.

As a result, increased urban population has led to increased demand for housing within the city, putting strain on the existing land apportioned for housing development. This has further necessitated encroachment on forest land reserves to provide space for housing development, hence urban extent expansion. Development of human settlement is a major cause of rapid land use/land cover changes within the Nairobi Metropolitan area.

Additionally, increased urban population has seen depletion of forest stock through deforestation in a bid to provide much-needed forest products like wood, medicine, food, and firewood for various human needs. This has resulted in shrinking and fragmentation of forest land reserves abating the City of Nairobi, and in this case Oloolua Forest.

As a result, land use/land cover management is a challenge to city managers due to lack of an up-to-date spatial plan; in most cases, corrective measures are applied after development has occurred. Consequently, it becomes difficult to ensure sustainable development while ensuring service provision to the urban population does not cause irreversible environmental impacts. Forests form a key part of land use/land cover for any liveable environment due to their various uses and benefits; hence the need for conservation.

Various studies have shown that geospatial technology can be used as an effective tool for monitoring land use/land cover changes, resource mapping, and land use planning. Mapping and tracking these changes is important for forest management decision-makers. GIS and Remote Sensing also provide capabilities for assessing the interaction of human activities on temporal and spatial scales, making them effective for decision-making based on precise quantifiable management practices confined to specific areas within the city or forest.

This study seeks to develop a well-coordinated geospatial-based framework for sustainable management of Oloolua Forest by:
- Establishing the extent of the forest reserve and mapping various human activities taking place within the forest.
- Determining the dynamics and trends of various human activities in relation to land use/land cover within Oloolua Forest over time from 1990 to 2020.
- Mapping existing tree species and coverage of tree stands within the forest.
- Identifying invasive species clusters and mapping their location.
- Developing integrated management practices and framework that ensure sustainable utilization of forest resources while enhancing conservation.

### 1.1 Problem Statement

Rapid urban population growth within Nairobi City County exerts immense pressure on finite land resources to provide space for expansion of human settlements. As a result, there is need for construction of housing and related infrastructure such as transportation, hence expansion of urban extent. In addition, increased consumption of forest-related products due to increased urban population demand has led to encroachment and fragmentation of forest reserves within Nairobi Metropolitan area, for instance Ngong Forest Reserve of which Oloolua Forest was part. This, coupled with lack of a well-coordinated forest management plan that integrates human development and sustainability of the forest as a resource, accelerates depletion of this forest.

### 1.2 Main Objective

The main aim of this research is to develop a geospatial-based framework for sustainable management of Oloolua Forest.

### 1.3 Specific Objectives

1. To provide a map of the forest including its boundary and the location of various activities taking place in Oloolua Forest.
2. To determine the dynamics and trends of various activities in Oloolua Forest over time for the period through 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020.
3. To identify various tree species and the coverage of stands within Oloolua Forest.
4. To identify location and type of invasive species clusters within Oloolua Forest.
5. To develop a management framework to enable sustainable utilization of forest resources while enhancing conservation.

### 1.4 Research Questions

1. Where are the various activities taking place within Oloolua Forest in relation to forest boundary extent?
2. What are the dynamics and trends of various activities in Oloolua Forest over time through 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020?
3. What is the stand coverage of the various tree species found within Oloolua Forest?
4. Where are the clusters of various invasive tree species located within Oloolua Forest?
5. Which management framework enables sustainable utilization of forest resources while enhancing conservation within Oloolua Forest?

### 1.5 Justification of the Study

Recent studies on forest conservation and management have emphasized only human activities as causes of forest depletion. These studies have focused mainly on qualitative assessment at global scale, whereas different ecosystems respond differently to various human influences. Quantification of these causes in terms of rates of effect on temporal and spatial scales has not been fully exploited to yield better statistics for management decisions.

This study shall utilize geospatial techniques in GIS and Remote Sensing which can yield quantifiable statistics on both spatial and temporal scales to inform patterns and relationships of human activities that affect forest management. Additionally, this study seeks to localize this assessment to Oloolua Forest so as to consider habitat uniqueness in development of proper management practices.

### 1.6 Significance of the Study

The increased rate of urban population growth within Nairobi has detrimental effects on its hinterland, as city dwellers occupy lands closest to the city, leading to shrinking and fragmentation of surrounding forests like Oloolua Forest. Continued human development through housing and transport infrastructure through the forest has led to heavy fragmentation and shrinking.

In addition, demand for construction material has led to trees being cut to give way for quarrying, encouraging deforestation. Trees are being cut for timber, charcoal, and firewood through illegal logging.

Little effort has been made to reclaim deforested lands, and people continue grabbing forest land for housing. Lack of a well-coordinated forest management plan that integrates human activities and forest sustainability accelerates forest depletion.

There is therefore need for a well-coordinated forest management plan that integrates utilization needs and sustainability capacity. The study is important in establishing resource location and characteristics needed to develop better management planning.

The study shall form a key reference document for the City County Land Development Board and KFS in regulating development within gazetted areas to ensure sustainability of Oloolua Forest. It shall also inform other scholarly work in related studies.

### 1.7 Limitation of the Study

- Some areas within the forest are inaccessible due to thick vegetation, making data collection on tree species challenging.
- Obtaining high-resolution satellite imagery for 2020 may be difficult due to acquisition cost.
- Delays in obtaining permits to traverse restricted forest areas may delay study delivery.
- Health hazards such as attack by wild animals in the forest can delay field work.

## 2.0 Methodology

### 2.1 Study Area

The study area is Oloolua Forest located along Easting 244591.23 and Northing 9849117.86 south of the Equator. It is situated approximately 20 kilometers south-west of Nairobi in the peri-urban area of Karen. It is an important wildlife refuge and biodiversity hotspot. The forest covers 618 hectares and is home to significant acreage of indigenous trees. The forest is part of the larger Ngong Forest block, which also comprises Ngong Hills and Kibiko Forest blocks. Oloolua acts as a vital wildlife corridor between Nairobi National Park and Ngong Hills.

### 2.2 Data Sources

The study will utilize both primary and secondary data sources:
- **Primary data**: field observation records, field GPS mapping records, and local interview feedback.
- **Secondary data**: remote-sensed satellite imagery, articles, publications, and books on the study theme.

### 2.3 Proposed Methodology

| Objective | Methodology |
|---|---|
| 1) To provide a map of forest boundary and activities | Georeference scanned KFS map in ArcGIS; digitize boundary; interview local communities; map activity locations with GPS and attributes; import GPS points to ArcGIS; create distribution map and compute area coverage statistics where possible. |
| 2) To determine dynamics and trends of activities over time | Download georeferenced Landsat imagery (cloud cover <10%); layer stack bands 2,3,4,5; clip to forest boundary; define classes (built-up, quarry, forest cover); generate training sites/signature file; apply supervised classification (Maximum Likelihood) in ERDAS Imagine; map epoch comparisons (1990/2000/2010/2020); compute area statistics, differences, and average rate of change; validate in field; discuss findings. |
| 3) To identify tree species and stand coverage | Conduct guided field identification and GPS mapping; create area training sites; stack natural color and NIR1 from WorldView-3; clip to forest boundary; create classification objects/signature file; apply GEOBIA region-growing for crown detection and delineation; map tree stands; infer planted vs natural stands; compute area statistics; discuss findings. |
| 4) To identify invasive species clusters | Subdivide area into 1 km square transects; sample investigation areas; field identification with local guide; GPS mapping of invasive species; import points to ArcMap; generate density map through interpolation; discuss findings. |
| 5) To develop management framework | Identify vulnerable areas/species; develop sustainable management plan covering endangered species conservation, rehabilitation of deforested areas, designated human-activity zones, designated patrol zones, integrated GIS web application for monitoring/utilization/management, and implementation matrix. |

## Literature Review

### 2.0 Literature Review

Urbanization stands as one of the most transformative global trends of the twenty-first century, drastically modifying landscapes and placing unprecedented pressure on natural ecosystems. In Sub-Saharan Africa, this phenomenon is particularly pronounced, characterized by rapid demographic shifts and peripheral urban sprawl that frequently compromises ecological reserves (Ndegwa et al., 2019).

This literature review contextualizes the spatial and ecological challenges facing peri-urban forests, using Oloolua Forest in the Nairobi Metropolitan Area as a focal case study. It synthesizes existing scholarly research on:
- Dynamics of urban growth and subsequent land use/land cover (LULC) modifications
- Role of geospatial technologies in monitoring these structural shifts
- Application of GEOBIA for precise floristic inventorying
- Spatial ecology of biological invasions
- Formulation of integrated geospatial frameworks for sustainable forest management

### 2.1 Urban Sprawl, Population Dynamics, and Forest Fragmentation

The relationship between accelerating urban demographics and preservation of contiguous forest canopies is inherently conflictual within developing economies. UN-Habitat (2021) highlighted Nairobi as one of the fastest-growing urban agglomerations on the African continent, driven by rural-urban migration, high fertility rates, and infrastructural centralization.

As metropolitan borders expand outward to accommodate population influx, land-strapped municipal centers experience severe peripheral sprawl. This horizontal expansion alters regional LULC profiles, converting agricultural and gazetted ecological reserves into residential and commercial real estate (Mundia & Aniya, 2010).

In peri-urban environments, ecosystems face dual pressures: direct spatial encroachment and intensified resource extraction. Urban dependencies on adjacent forest stock lead to systematic degradation through localized deforestation for fuel wood, timber, and charcoal production, alongside illegal land allocation (Mideksa & Korme, 2020).

Ecologically, this not only reduces total forest area but drives severe habitat fragmentation. Fragmentation breaks down extensive, continuous forest ecosystems into isolated smaller patches (Ngugi et al., 2021). This structural disruption triggers edge effects that alter microclimatic conditions and reduce core habitat for sensitive indigenous species while opening pathways for opportunistic taxa.

### 2.2 Geospatial Applications in Monitoring Temporal LULC Changes

Traditional ground-based forestry assessments are resource-intensive and often lack temporal frequency needed to capture rapid urban encroachment. Remote Sensing (RS) and GIS provide a robust alternative through quantifiable, objective, and spatially explicit observations across time (Hussain et al., 2013).

For multi-decadal ecosystem analysis, open-access archival satellite constellations—especially USGS-NASA Landsat—provide an invaluable baseline for historical LULC reconstruction since the 1970s at 30 m resolution (Gorelick et al., 2017).

When extracting land cover classes (built-up, quarry, closed-canopy forest), pixel-based digital image classification remains foundational. The Maximum Likelihood Classification (MLC) algorithm is widely used due to reliable performance within parametric constraints (Hussain et al., 2013; Mideksa & Korme, 2020).

However, standard pixel-based approaches face limitations in complex urban-forest interfaces. Landsat's 30 m pixels can produce mixed pixels along forest boundaries and transport corridors, introducing spectral blending errors. This underscores the need for post-classification validation using ground-truthing and confusion matrix assessments (Ndegwa et al., 2019).

### 2.3 Advanced Remote Sensing: GEOBIA for Species and Stand Mapping

While medium-resolution imagery is effective for long-term regional LULC trends, mapping individual tree species and stand densities requires high spatial resolution datasets such as WorldView-3 imagery. Applying traditional pixel-based classification at sub-meter scale often causes the salt-and-pepper effect where intra-crown variability drives pixel misclassification (Blaschke et al., 2014).

To address this limitation, Geographic Object-Based Image Analysis (GEOBIA) groups contiguous pixels into spectrally and spatially homogeneous image objects through segmentation (Blaschke et al., 2014).

For forest inventories, region-growing algorithms are effective for tree crown detection and delineation by expanding from seed pixels until a spectral or structural boundary is reached.
